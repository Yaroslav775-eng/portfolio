The Starting Point: A large catalog and demand that could not be managed as one flat campaign

The project sits in a category where search demand is highly specific. Users are not simply looking for “wheel caps”, but for a product that matches a particular car brand, wheel style, or accessory type. That is visible even on the site structure itself: demand is split by brands, which means the ad account cannot be run as one undifferentiated block without losing control.

In e-commerce projects like this, the challenge is rarely “not enough advertising”. The challenge is budget dilution. Some product groups can produce strong value consistently, others drive traffic with weaker commercial impact, and still others need a different role in the account. Without the right structure, scaling starts to hurt unit economics instead of helping them.

The Key Shift

The job was not to increase visibility in the abstract. The job was to create a controllable paid acquisition system where it was clear which parts of the catalog could scale cleanly and where additional reach would begin to damage efficiency.

What Changed

Work in projects like this is never about one button or one campaign type. It is about architecture. The goal was to make the catalog manageable through advertising, while making demand cleaner and easier to scale profitably.

The New Operating Model

The account needed to behave not like a flat list of products, but like a system where advertising understood the difference between demand layers, category roles, and the commercial function of each product group.

  • Catalog segmentation. Different groups of products needed to be analyzed and scaled separately instead of being blended into one budget pool.
  • Demand separation. Branded and category-level scenarios require different advertising behavior. That allows search intent to be matched more precisely to the right product group.
  • Structure over raw reach. In a large catalog, the goal is not just more visibility, but visibility that does not break efficiency.
  • Controlled scaling. Budget decisions should not follow a flat “push more everywhere” logic. They should follow which categories can actually sustain more conversion value.
  • Business-first measurement. Performance is judged through the relationship between spend, conversion value, and actual ROAS, not through surface-level engagement metrics alone.

Why structure matters so much in this kind of niche

In catalog e-commerce, Google Ads often looks fine on the surface: impressions, traffic, clicks. But real performance begins when the account becomes governable. The real value in this case was making the account capable of growth through a clearer economic logic instead of through broad exposure alone.

When demand is spread across brands, categories, and specific purchase scenarios, advertising needs to separate high-value parts of the catalog from secondary ones. That is what turns paid traffic from activity into a repeatable growth model.

The Outcome: Strong conversion value and a working scale model

From July 1, 2025 through June 14, 2026, the account generated 11.7M impressions, UAH 8.81M in conversion value, UAH 851k in spend, and an actual ROAS of 1034.33%.

Those numbers matter not because they look impressive in a dashboard, but because they indicate that the advertising system was producing business value with strong payback. For an e-commerce business, that means the account was not merely active. It was structurally usable for further scaling without losing control.

What this case proved

  • In large-catalog e-commerce, results depend on architecture as much as on budget.
  • Wide assortments cannot be scaled with one flat logic.
  • Category and brand demand need to be separated so strong product groups do not subsidize weak ones.
  • True scaling begins when the account is managed through conversion value and ROAS, not through traffic volume alone.

Is your e-commerce growth driven by structure or just by spend?

If you run a large catalog and Google Ads is active but scaling still feels hard to control, the issue is often account structure, demand segmentation, and the role assigned to each product group.
